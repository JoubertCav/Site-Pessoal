Convex functions and epigraphs

A convex function bends below its chords. In one higher dimension, this becomes an ordinary set-theoretic statement: the region above the graph is convex. The same geometry controls Jensen’s inequality, sublevel sets, continuity, and lower semicontinuity.

Necessary, not sufficient

Convex functions have convex sublevel sets. The converse fails: a function may be quasiconvex, with every sublevel set convex, without satisfying Jensen’s inequality.

Lower semicontinuity

A function is lower semicontinuous exactly when every sublevel set \(\{x:f(x)\le\alpha\}\) is closed, equivalently when its epigraph is closed.

7 · Continuity of convex functions

Convexity prevents interior oscillation

A proper convex function is continuous on the relative interior of its effective domain.

Theorem

At boundary points of the domain, continuity may fail or the value may be \(+\infty\).

One-dimensional idea

Secant slopes are monotone

Local consequence

Bounded slopes on compact subintervals

The function is locally Lipschitz away from the boundary of its domain.

Higher dimensions

Restrict to line segments

Continuity follows by controlling the function along finitely many directions inside a small simplex.

8 · Closed epigraph theorem

Lower semicontinuity is geometric closedness

The theorem connects a sequential limit condition, closed sublevel sets, and a closed set in one higher dimension.

Let \(x_n\to x\) and choose a subsequence along which \(f(x_n)\to\liminf f(x_n)\). If this limit is finite, the epigraph points \((x_n,f(x_n))\) converge to \((x,\ell)\). Closedness gives \(\ell\ge f(x)\).

If \((x_n,r_n)\in\operatorname{epi}f\) and \((x_n,r_n)\to(x,r)\), then \(f(x_n)\le r_n\). Hence

Therefore \((x,r)\in\operatorname{epi}f\).

Proof strategy

Encode values as points

An upward spike at the limit point creates a convergent sequence of epigraph points whose limit lies below the graph, exactly violating closedness.

10 · What can go wrong?

Nearby properties are not equivalent

Convexity, quasiconvexity, continuity, and lower semicontinuity form distinct logical layers.

Convex sublevel sets do not imply convexity

The increasing function \(f(x)=x^3\) has interval sublevel sets on \(\mathbb R\), yet it is not convex on all of \(\mathbb R\).

Correct concept. Such a function is quasiconvex, not necessarily convex.

Continuity is guaranteed only on the relative interior

An extended-real indicator of a closed convex set is convex and lower semicontinuous, but it jumps to \(+\infty\) outside the set.

Correct domain. Continuity is asserted on \(\operatorname{ri}(\operatorname{dom}f)\).

A convex function need not be closed at a boundary point

On \([0,\infty)\), define \(f(0)=1\) and \(f(x)=0\) for \(x>0\). This function is convex but not lower semicontinuous at \(0\).

Geometric defect. The epigraph misses the limit point \((0,0)\).

Closed epigraph does not imply convexity

Every continuous nonconvex function, such as \(f(x)=\sin x\), has a closed epigraph.

Distinct roles. Closedness corresponds to lower semicontinuity; convexity corresponds to epigraph convexity.
11 · Common mistakes

Precision traps

Most errors confuse a geometric property of the epigraph with a topological property of the graph.

Incorrect: “The graph of a convex function is a convex set.” Correct: the epigraph is convex; the graph usually is not.
Incorrect: “Convexity means the graph lies above every chord.” Correct: the graph lies below or on every chord.
Incorrect: “Convex sublevel sets characterize convex functions.” Correct: they characterize quasiconvexity.
Incorrect: “Every finite convex function is automatically continuous everywhere.” Correct on open domains; more generally, continuity holds on the relative interior of the effective domain.
Incorrect: “Lower semicontinuity forbids downward spikes.” Correct: downward spikes are allowed; an upward spike at the limit point violates the liminf inequality.
