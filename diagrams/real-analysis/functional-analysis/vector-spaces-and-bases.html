What can go wrong?

Counterexamples that isolate the hypotheses

Near-misses are useful because they reveal which part of a definition performs the real work.

Spanning does not imply basis

The family spans, but it is dependent. The third vector is redundant, so coordinate representations are not unique.

Independence does not imply spanning

The vectors are independent, but they generate only the plane , not all of .

A quotient vector is not a chosen representative

No representative is intrinsically preferred. A quotient point is the entire coset, not one vector drawn from it.

Finite intuition can fail in infinite dimension

No finite subfamily spans all polynomials. The space has infinite dimension, so finite termination arguments must be used carefully.

Common mistakes

Logical distinctions worth protecting

“A spanning family gives unique coordinates.”
False. Spanning gives existence. Uniqueness requires linear independence. A basis is exactly the combination of both properties.
“To prove dependence, every coefficient must be nonzero.”
False. One nontrivial coefficient family is enough: at least one coefficient must be nonzero, not all of them.
“A quotient space removes the subspace from the ambient space.”
False. The quotient does not delete points. It identifies vectors whose difference lies in the subspace, replacing each coset by a single abstract point.
“Dimension is the number of vectors in any generating family.”
False. Generating families may contain redundancy. Dimension is the number of vectors in a basis, equivalently the maximal size of an independent family in finite dimension.
