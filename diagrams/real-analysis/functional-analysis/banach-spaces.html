1. Formal core

What completeness says

Completeness is not a property of a sequence alone. It is a property of the ambient normed space: every Cauchy sequence must converge to an element of that same space.

Definition — Banach space
\[ (X,\|\cdot\|)\text{ is Banach} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \forall (x_n)\subset X, \quad (x_n)\text{ Cauchy} \Longrightarrow \exists x\in X:\ \|x_n-x\|\to 0. \]
Universal objectevery sequence \((x_n)\subset X\)
Hypothesisthe sequence is Cauchy
Existencethere is a limit \(x\)
Membershipthe limit satisfies \(x\in X\)
Conclusion\(\|x_n-x\|\to0\)
Cauchy condition
\[ \forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists N\in\mathbb N\;\forall m,n\ge N, \qquad \|x_n-x_m\|<\varepsilon. \]

The tail terms become mutually close. No candidate limit is named.

Convergence
\[ \exists x\in X\;\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists N\in\mathbb N\;\forall n\ge N, \qquad \|x_n-x\|<\varepsilon. \]

The tail terms approach one fixed point that must belong to the space.

4. Canonical examples

Where completeness lives

These examples are Banach for different structural reasons: coordinate completeness, pointwise construction plus uniform estimates, or absolute summability.

Finite dimensional

\(\mathbb R^d\) under any norm

\[x_n\text{ Cauchy}\Longrightarrow x_n\to x\in\mathbb R^d.\]
Every norm is equivalent to the Euclidean norm.
Coordinatewise completeness gives the limit.
Finite-dimensional subspaces of normed spaces are closed.
Bounded functions

\(B(X)\) with \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\)

\[\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|.\]
A sup-norm Cauchy sequence is pointwise Cauchy.
The pointwise limit is bounded.
The convergence is uniform.
Sequence spaces

\(\ell^p\), \(1\le p\le\infty\)

\[\|x\|_p=\Big(\sum_{k=1}^\infty |x_k|^p\Big)^{1/p},\quad 1\le p<\infty,\qquad \|x\|_\infty=\sup_k|x_k|.\]
Cauchy control in norm forces coordinatewise limits.
Fatou-type estimates keep the limit in \(\ell^p\).
The norm distance to the limit tends to zero.
Continuous functions

\(C(K)\) with the sup norm

When \(K\) is compact, every continuous function is bounded, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. Hence \(C(K)\) is a closed subspace of \(B(K)\), therefore Banach.

Closed subspaces

Closed subspace theorem

\[Y\subset X,\quad X\text{ Banach},\quad Y\text{ closed}\Longrightarrow Y\text{ Banach}.\]

A Cauchy sequence in \(Y\) converges in \(X\); closedness forces the limit back into \(Y\).

5. Proof architecture

Why \(B(X)\) is Banach

The decisive move is to use the same Cauchy index uniformly for every point of the domain. Pointwise limits alone are not enough.

Step-by-step proof navigator
Let \((f_n)\) be Cauchy in \((B(X),\|\cdot\|_\infty)\). Thus, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(N\) such that \[m,n\ge N\Longrightarrow \sup_{x\in X}|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|<\varepsilon.\] Used: the sup-norm Cauchy hypothesis.
Fix \(x\in X\). Then \[|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\le \|f_n-f_m\|_\infty,\] so \((f_n(x))\) is Cauchy in \(\mathbb R\). Since \(\mathbb R\) is complete, define \[f(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(x).\] Used: completeness of the scalar field.
Fix \(\varepsilon>0\), and choose \(N\) so that \(m,n\ge N\) implies \(\|f_n-f_m\|_\infty<\varepsilon/2\). For \(n\ge N\) and every \(x\in X\), let \(m\to\infty\) in \[|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.\] This gives \(|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon\) for every \(x\), hence \[\|f_n-f\|_\infty\le\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<\varepsilon.\] Crucial: one index \(N\) works simultaneously for all \(x\).
Choose one index \(n_0\) with \(\|f-f_{n_0}\|_\infty<1\). Then \[\|f\|_\infty\le \|f-f_{n_0}\|_\infty+\|f_{n_0}\|_\infty<1+\|f_{n_0}\|_\infty.\] Thus \(f\in B(X)\).
We constructed \(f\in B(X)\) and proved \(\|f_n-f\|_\infty\to0\). Therefore every Cauchy sequence in \(B(X)\) converges in \(B(X)\), so \(B(X)\) is Banach.
Proof strategy

Pointwise construction + uniform recovery

Use the norm inequality to make each scalar sequence Cauchy.
Use completeness of \(\mathbb R\) to define the candidate limit.
Return to the original uniform estimate before taking the limit.
Verify that the limit lies in the same function space.
Common proof failure.
Pointwise convergence \(f_n(x)\to f(x)\) does not by itself imply \(\|f_n-f\|_\infty\to0\). The Cauchy estimate must be uniform in \(x\).
Immediate consequence — continuous bounded functions
\[ C_b(X)\text{ is closed in }B(X)\text{ under }\|\cdot\|_\infty \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_b(X)\text{ is Banach}. \]

Indeed, a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, and the proof above already guarantees boundedness and uniform convergence. For compact \(K\), this specializes to the familiar Banach space \(C(K)\).

6. What can go wrong?

Limits that escape the space

An incomplete normed space can contain every term of a Cauchy sequence while excluding the limit selected by the surrounding Banach space.

Smooth approximants
\[f_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\frac1n}.\]

Every \(f_n\) is continuously differentiable, but the uniform limit is \(|x|\), which is not differentiable at \(0\).

Exact obstruction
The sequence is Cauchy in the sup norm because it converges uniformly in \(C[-1,1]\). Its limit does not belong to \(C^1[-1,1]\). Therefore \(C^1[-1,1]\), equipped only with \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\), is not complete.
Finite-support truncations
\[x^{(n)}=(2^{-1},2^{-2},\ldots,2^{-n},0,0,\ldots).\]
Escaping limit
Each truncation lies in \(c_{00}\), but the \(\ell^1\)-limit \((2^{-k})_{k\ge1}\) has infinitely many nonzero coordinates. Hence \(c_{00}\) is dense in \(\ell^1\) but not closed, and therefore not Banach.
Polynomial subspace
\[ \mathcal P[-1,1]\subset C[-1,1], \qquad \|p\|_\infty=\max_{|x|\le1}|p(x)|. \]

By the Weierstrass approximation theorem, there exists a polynomial sequence \((p_n)\) converging uniformly to \(|x|\). Thus \((p_n)\) is Cauchy in the sup norm.

Why the proof breaks
\[p_n\in\mathcal P[-1,1],\qquad p_n\to |x|\notin\mathcal P[-1,1].\]
The ambient space \(C[-1,1]\) supplies the uniform limit, but the polynomial subspace is not closed. The missing hypothesis in the closed-subspace theorem is precisely closedness.

8. Common mistakes

Distinctions that proofs depend on

Incorrect: Every bounded sequence in a Banach space converges.

Completeness concerns Cauchy sequences, not arbitrary bounded sequences. The standard basis \((e_n)\) in \(\ell^2\) is bounded but satisfies \(\|e_n-e_m\|_2=\sqrt2\) for \(n\ne m\).

Correct: Every Cauchy sequence in a Banach space converges in norm.
Incorrect: A pointwise Cauchy sequence of bounded functions is Cauchy in the sup norm.

Pointwise control allows the index to depend on \(x\). Sup-norm Cauchy behavior requires one index working simultaneously over the whole domain.

Correct: Sup-norm Cauchy implies pointwise Cauchy, but the converse can fail.
Incorrect: Every subspace of a Banach space is Banach.

A subspace is Banach under the inherited norm exactly when it is closed. Polynomial functions and \(c_{00}\) are standard nonclosed subspaces.

Correct: Closed subspaces of Banach spaces are Banach.
Incorrect: Completeness is topological and therefore unchanged under any equivalent-looking metric.

Completeness is a uniform property. Equivalent norms preserve it because they quantitatively compare distances, but arbitrary metrics inducing the same topology need not preserve completeness.

Correct: Equivalent norms preserve Cauchy sequences and completeness.
Incorrect: If every Cauchy sequence has a pointwise limit, the function space is complete.

The limit must belong to the same space and convergence must occur in the specified norm.

Correct: Always verify existence, membership, and convergence in the ambient norm.
